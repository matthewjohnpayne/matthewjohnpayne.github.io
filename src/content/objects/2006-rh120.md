On 14 September 2006 the Catalina Sky Survey's 0.68-m Schmidt telescope in Arizona recorded a faint, fast-moving object. Over the following months it was followed by the Siding Spring Survey, Spacewatch, the Mt. Lemmon Survey, the 10-m Southern African Large Telescope and several smaller telescopes, and in June 2007 by the Goldstone radar ([MPEC 2008-D12](https://www.minorplanetcenter.net/mpec/K08/K08D12.html); [observatory codes](https://www.minorplanetcenter.net/iau/lists/ObsCodesF.html)). When the MPC published the observations in February 2008, they had been fitted with an orbit, computed by P. Chodas at JPL, that includes the small push of sunlight ([MPEC 2008-D12](https://www.minorplanetcenter.net/mpec/K08/K08D12.html)). The object is still unnumbered, so the MPC has not yet credited a discoverer ([MPC](https://www.minorplanetcenter.net/db_search/show_object?object_id=2006+RH120)).

## A year as a moon

From July 2006 to July 2007, 2006 RH<sub>120</sub> orbited the Earth within its Hill sphere, the region in which the Earth's gravity, rather than the Sun's, controls the motion of a small body ([Kwiatkowski et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361:200810965)). It was not a permanent moon. Like the Earth, it travels round the Sun on a nearly circular orbit of almost the same size and tilt (table below), so it can drift past the Earth slowly enough to be captured; after about a year it slipped away again. The figures below follow it through the capture in a frame that turns with the Earth, so that the Sun stays in one direction.

It is tiny. Observing it on four nights in March 2007 with the Southern African Large Telescope, [Kwiatkowski et al. (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361:200810965) measured an absolute magnitude H = 29.9 ± 0.3, which means a diameter of only 2–7 m, depending on how reflective it is, and a rotation period of either 1.375 or 2.75 minutes. Its lightcurve implies an elongated shape. They concluded that it probably came from a low-eccentricity Amor or Apollo orbit, among the near-Earth asteroids.

## The first of many

[Granvik, Vaubaillon & Jedicke (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.icarus.2011.12.003) calculated how often the Earth should capture small near-Earth objects this way. They found that at any time there should be at least one natural Earth satellite about 1 m across orbiting the Earth, and that a typical temporarily captured orbiter makes about three revolutions around the Earth during a capture that lasts about 290 days. At the time, 2006 RH<sub>120</sub> was the only one known, and their results were consistent with it. A second, 2020 CD<sub>3</sub>, was announced by the MPC in February 2020 after orbit integrations showed it to be temporarily bound to the Earth, with no link to any known artificial object ([MPEC 2020-D104](https://www.minorplanetcenter.net/mpec/K20/K20DA4.html)).
