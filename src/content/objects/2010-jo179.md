2010 JO<sub>179</sub> was discovered in images taken on 10 May 2010 by the Pan-STARRS 1 telescope on Haleakalā, Maui ([MPC](https://www.minorplanetcenter.net/db_search/show_object?object_id=2010+JO179)). Matt Holman and I, with Wesley Fraser, Pedro Lacerda, Michele Bannister and Michael Lackner, reported it in 2018 ([Holman et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/aaadb3)). It turned up in our search of the Pan-STARRS 1 survey for slow-moving distant objects, in 24 detections on 12 nights between 2010 and 2012, when it was about 55 au from the Sun. We then found it in archival images from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (2005) and the DECam Legacy Survey (2014), and observed it again with the New Technology Telescope in Chile in 2016 ([arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.05427)). The MPC's orbit now uses observations going back to February 1951, the year of its last perihelion ([MPC](https://www.minorplanetcenter.net/db_search/show_object?object_id=2010+JO179)).

## A dwarf planet candidate

2010 JO<sub>179</sub> has an absolute magnitude H<sub>r</sub> = 3.4 ± 0.1. It is red, roughly round and slowly rotating, with a period of 30.6 hours, and estimates of its albedo put its diameter between 600 and 900 km, large enough to make it a dwarf planet candidate ([Holman et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/aaadb3)).

## Locked to Neptune

Its orbit is long and tilted: it swings between about 39 and 117 au from the Sun, inclined by 32° to the plane of Earth's orbit, and takes about 690 years to go round (table below). That is very nearly 4.2 times Neptune's period, so in the time Neptune makes 21 orbits, 2010 JO<sub>179</sub> makes 5: a 21:5 mean-motion resonance.

A period ratio close to a simple fraction is not enough on its own. In a resonance, the gravitational tugs from Neptune keep the two orbits in step, and the sign of that is a *resonant angle*, a combination of the two bodies' positions and the orientation of the orbit, that oscillates (librates) about a fixed value instead of running through all values. Fitting six oppositions of observations between 2005 and 2016, we found a semi-major axis of 78.307 ± 0.009 au, an exceptionally precise orbit for so distant an object, and integrations of that orbit and of clones consistent with its uncertainties showed it librating securely in the 21:5 resonance on hundred-million-year timescales ([Holman et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/aaadb3)). Over the 700 million years of those integrations the orbits slip in and out of the resonance, which makes it *metastable*: 2010 JO<sub>179</sub> is one of a small but growing set of distant dwarf planets on such orbits, which implies a substantial population that shifts between high-order resonances, the detached population and the scattering disk ([arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.05427)).
