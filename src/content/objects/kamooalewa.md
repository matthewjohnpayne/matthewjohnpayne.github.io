Kamoʻoalewa was found on 27 April 2016 by the Pan-STARRS 1 telescope on Haleakalā, Maui, and given the provisional designation 2016 HO<sub>3</sub> ([MPC](https://www.minorplanetcenter.net/db_search/show_object?object_id=469219)). In 2019 Hawaiian-language students named it through the A Hua He Inoa programme at the ʻImiloa Astronomy Center in Hilo. In ʻōlelo Hawaiʻi the name alludes to a celestial object that oscillates, like the asteroid's path in the sky as seen from Earth ([UH News](https://www.hawaii.edu/news/2026/06/05/uh-discovered-kamooalewa/)).

## An orbit almost the same as Earth's

Kamoʻoalewa orbits the Sun, not the Earth. Its orbital period is about 365.7 days, within a few hours of Earth's year, so the two travel round the Sun in step: a 1:1 mean-motion resonance. Its orbit is a little more eccentric than Earth's and tilted by about 8 degrees, so over each year it drifts ahead of Earth and behind it, inside Earth's orbit and outside it. Seen from a frame that turns with the Earth, that drift traces a loop which encloses the Earth (second figure below). This is what makes it a *quasi-satellite*: it appears to circle the Earth, but it stays far outside the region where Earth's gravity could hold it, and the loop is really an effect of the turning frame. The [reference-frames page](/teaching/reference-frames/) explains the loop and lets you watch it from five points of view.

The arrangement is not permanent. Kamoʻoalewa switches between this quasi-satellite state and a *horseshoe* orbit, in which it drifts slowly back and forth along Earth's orbit. [de la Fuente Marcos & de la Fuente Marcos (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stw1972) found that the present quasi-satellite episode began nearly 100 years ago and will end in about 300 years, and that it may stay a companion of the Earth for millions of years. Including the Yarkovsky effect, the slow push from sunlight absorbed and re-radiated by a small body, [Fenucci & Novaković (2021)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-3881/ac2902) found it could remain an Earth companion for at least another half a million years, making it the most stable Earth co-orbital known.

## A piece of the Moon?

Kamoʻoalewa is small: its absolute magnitude H is about 24.3 (table below), and estimates of its diameter range from about 36 m ([Fenucci & Novaković 2021](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-3881/ac2902)) to 26–115 m ([de la Fuente Marcos & de la Fuente Marcos 2016](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stw1972)). Observing it with the Large Binocular Telescope and the Lowell Discovery Telescope, [Sharkey et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43247-021-00303-7) measured a rotation period of about 28 minutes and a reddened silicate spectrum whose best match among the materials they compared was lunar-like silicates. That raised the possibility that Kamoʻoalewa is lunar material.

Simulations of fragments thrown off the Moon by impacts found that a small fraction can reach orbits like Kamoʻoalewa's ([Castro-Cisneros, Malhotra & Rosengren 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43247-023-01031-w)). [Jiao et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41550-024-02258-z) went further, proposing the young lunar crater Giordano Bruno, 22 km across and 1–10 million years old, as the most likely source.

## A visit

China's Tianwen-2 mission, launched on 28 May 2025 on a Long March 3B rocket from Xichang ([Sky & Telescope](https://skyandtelescope.org/astronomy-news/china-launches-tianwen-2-asteroid-sample-return-mission/); [Planetary Radio](https://www.planetary.org/planetary-radio/2026-tianwen-2)), was due to begin its encounter with Kamoʻoalewa in July 2026 ([UH News](https://www.hawaii.edu/news/2026/06/05/uh-discovered-kamooalewa/)). It plans to return a sample capsule to Earth on 29 November 2027 and then travel on to comet 311P ([Sky & Telescope](https://skyandtelescope.org/astronomy-news/china-launches-tianwen-2-asteroid-sample-return-mission/)). A sample in the laboratory would test the lunar-origin idea directly.
