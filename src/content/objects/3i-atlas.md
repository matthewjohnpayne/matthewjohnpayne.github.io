On 1 July 2025 the ATLAS survey telescope at Río Hurtado in Chile picked up a new moving object in four 30-second survey images, and it was posted on the MPC's NEO Confirmation Page. Follow-up and earlier ("precovery") observations, some from June, quickly showed an orbit that was not just unbound but extremely hyperbolic, with an eccentricity of about 6, and there were tentative reports of a faint coma and a short tail. The MPC announced it the next day as 3I/ATLAS, also designated C/2025 N1 (ATLAS) ([MPEC 2025-N12](https://www.minorplanetcenter.net/mpec/K25/K25N12.html)): the third interstellar object, after 1I/ʻOumuamua and 2I/Borisov, in the series of "I" numbers introduced in 2017, after discussions between the IAU and the MPC, which assigns them ([MPEC 2017-V17](https://minorplanetcenter.net/mpec/K17/K17V17.html)). Pre-discovery observations from other ATLAS telescopes and the Zwicky Transient Facility reach back to 14 June 2025 ([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/comets/3i-atlas/)), and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, still being commissioned, had imaged it from 21 June, when it was 4.5 au from the Sun ([Chandler et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/ae4b3a)).

## Just passing through

A body bound to the Sun has an eccentricity below 1. 3I/ATLAS's is about 6.1, its orbit is inclined by about 175°, so it travels round the Sun in the opposite direction to the planets, and far from the Sun it moves at about 58 km/s ([Seligman et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adf49a); table below). Traced back in time, its path clearly comes from outside the Solar System. It reached perihelion around 30 October 2025, about 1.4 au from the Sun, just inside the orbit of Mars, and came no closer to the Earth than about 1.8 au: it posed no threat ([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/comets/3i-atlas/)). The figures below follow its path through the planets.

Near perihelion it was too close to the Sun in the sky for telescopes on the ground: it was observable until September 2025 and reappeared in early December ([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/comets/3i-atlas/)). Spacecraft filled the gap. Its path took it close to Mars in early October, and NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, MAVEN and the Perseverance rover all observed it, as did the Psyche, Lucy, Europa Clipper and Parker Solar Probe missions, among others ([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/comets/3i-atlas/)).

## A comet from somewhere colder

3I/ATLAS is active: deep images from the Canada–France–Hawaii Telescope and the Very Large Telescope resolved a compact coma soon after discovery, and the slope of its visible and near-infrared spectrum is comparable to those of the other interstellar objects and of primitive Solar System bodies such as comets ([Seligman et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adf49a)). Its gas is less familiar. The water in 3I/ATLAS is enriched in deuterium, with D/H of about 1%, more than ten times the level in known comets, and its carbon isotope ratios are also unusual. [Cordiner et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10771-6) read these as signs that it formed at temperatures below about 30 K in a relatively metal-poor environment, perhaps as long as 12 billion years ago.

More are expected. Devin Hoover, Darryl Seligman and I estimated that the Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time should detect of order 15 interstellar objects over its roughly ten-year campaign ([Hoover, Seligman & Payne 2022](https://doi.org/10.3847/PSJ/ac58fe)); the [interactive page](/teaching/interstellar-objects/) shows where in the sky they should appear.
